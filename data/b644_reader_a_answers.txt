ANSWER 1:
A hinge is a premise (a named, assumed Prop that some theorems rest on) whose consumers, meaning the theorems whose statement or proof uses it as read from the compiled dependency data, fall in more than one kernel (Lean repository), or in more than one "chain" inside one kernel. A chain is a connected piece of the kernel's use-graph made up of the declarations that depend on the premise. The document gives two definitions. The older one (v0.7, glossary line 89, back matter line 1148) is the bare rule above, and under it 28 of the 50 premise heads were hinges. The current, refined one (v0.7.1, ruling R254, glossary line 90 and back matter line 1221) changes it in two ways. First, before chains are counted, the premise's "own evidence" is removed from the graph: Salt witnesses, refutations of the premise, and theorems whose conclusion is the premise itself, plus its projections and constructors. Second, only a premise with one of the five programme statuses (OPEN, CITED, DISCHARGED, WITNESSED, REFUTED-BY-COMPUTATION) can be a hinge. A DOMAIN premise, which is a Mathlib predicate restricting a quantified variable, is printed with its chains but is not a hinge.

Every one of the 14 qualifies because it has several chains inside a single kernel. None qualifies by spanning kernels. The only premise whose consumers span several kernels is Prime, and it is DOMAIN, so it is excluded. Compared with v0.7's list of 28, 16 heads drop out.
